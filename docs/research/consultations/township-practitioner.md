## Part 1

1. The biggest headache is creating a township that remains economically, socially, and environmentally vibrant after the developer has largely exited.
2. The biggest hidden challenges are not construction, but regulatory approvals and compliance obligations. Lengthy authority approval processes increase holding and finance costs, while statutory contributions for infrastructure and public facilities significantly impact project viability.
3. The biggest challenge is the township's long-term sustainability. Once homes are handed over, ownership becomes fragmented. While residents, local authorities, and management bodies each manage a small part, nobody truly owns the township's long-term economy vitality.
4. The student team should focus on improving community engagement, such as to develop a simple digital platform that connects residents, encourages participation in community activities, and makes it easier to report local issues.
5. One idea that has been proposed many times but rarely works sustainably in practice is community-led gotong-royong programmes. The lesson is that sustainability cannot rely solely on goodwill. It requires a combination of community participation, institutional support, funding mechanisms, and clear accountability.

## Part 2

**A (drains and ponds after handover).** The bigger issue is not who owns the drain or pond after handover, but whether there is clear accountability for maintenance and public safety. Sustainable townships require continuous collaboration between the developer, local authority, JPS and residents. Even after handover, developers who take proactive measures, such as monitoring flood-prone areas, providing warning signage, and supporting mitigation works, often help preserve community trust and township value.

**B (independent monitoring near data centres).** Yes, I believe developers should welcome independent noise and dust monitoring because it promotes transparency, regulatory compliance, and public confidence. Residents living near large developments are typically most concerned about construction dust, noise, traffic congestion, and potential impacts on their health and quality of life. Some residents may also have concerns about electromagnetic or radio frequency exposure from data centre operations, so developers should proactively engage the community, share monitoring results, and provide clear factual info to address misconceptions and build trust.

**C (buyers, bills and smart air-conditioning).** Buyers are increasingly conscious of utility costs, particularly electricity bills. While not all buyers actively ask for solar systems, there is growing interest in energy-saving solutions. Developers can respond by offering smart home features such as intelligent air-conditioning controls, energy monitoring systems or solar-ready infrastructure, creating both cost savings for residents and a stronger sustainability proposition for the township.

## Follow-ups

**Who pays for upkeep and safety fixes after handover?** "The local town council will normally impose contributions, such as an Improvement Service Fund, on developers, and this fund is usually used to perform all sorts of maintenance for public facilities etc."

**Can ISF money pay for safety fixes after handover (rails, ledges, lighting), or only new works?** "For post-township development maintenance work."

Sources the practitioner shared, checked on 16 October 2026:

- EdgeProp (The Edge Financial Daily pullout, pages EP6 and EP7), "Compliance cost and what it means" by Shawn Ng, 14 December 2018 in print and 15 December 2018 online: the ISF is "a fund established in each local authority to collect funds for the purpose of the beautification, construction or laying out of any street, drain, culvert, gutter or water-course", under the Street, Drainage and Building Act 1974. <https://www.edgeprop.my/content/1454158/compliance-cost-and-what-it-means>
- The Star, "Improvement Service Fund imposed on developers to balance development in Petaling Jaya" by Edward Rajendra, 27 January 2014: Petaling Jaya developers pay into the ISF "to offset the impact the new development has on the area"; MBPJ uses it to "improve basic amenities such as roads". <https://www.thestar.com.my/News/Community/2014/01/27/Boosting-PJs-amenities-Improvement-Service-Fund-imposed-on-developers-to-balance-development/>
- The Star via PressReader, 11 November 2025: a different article; could not be opened (HTTP 403) and its headline is not known. <https://www.pressreader.com/malaysia/the-star-malaysia/20251111/282638923848864>

Our own check: MBDK's ISF checklist charges a drainage ISF of RM5,000 per acre of development to the original developer, paid in full before planning permission is confirmed, into the council's ISF trust fund (Kumpulan Wang Amanah ISF). <https://www.mbdk.gov.my/sites/default/files/media/document/2024-03/9.%20SENARAI%20SEMAK%20IMPROVEMENT%20SERVICE%20FUND%20%28ISF%29.pdf>

Analyst's read: the statutory wording covers construction and laying out of drains and water-courses, and the practitioner says councils use it for post-development maintenance. No public source found shows how a council spends ISF money edge by edge.

The print EdgeProp article (two scanned pages the practitioner shared) adds MKH Bhd's sample cost breakdown for a 500-unit apartment project on 5 acres in the Klang Valley, from MKH's report "The Impact of Development Contributions & Costs Associated with Planning Intervention":

- ISF contribution for roads: RM500 per 1,000 sq ft, RM225,000 in the example. ISF contribution for drainage: RM5,000 per acre, RM25,000 in the example (matches MBDK's checklist).
- Total capital contributions and compliance costs: RM8,231,200, or 6.6% of the RM125 million GDV. MKH's Tan Sri Eddy Chen says these charges can range from 8% to 20% of GDV.
- Residential ISF rose from RM0 in 2008 to RM250 per unit (city or municipal council) and RM500 per unit (district council) in 2018 (Table 2, source MKH).

Analyst's read: this supports the practitioner's point that compliance and statutory contributions weigh on project viability, and shows that developers already pay for drain upkeep through the ISF without seeing where it goes.
